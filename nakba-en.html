The Nakba 1948: “Flight” – or Expulsion?

Between December 1947 and 1949 the majority of the Palestinian population of what became Israel’s state territory was expelled from their homes or driven to flee by attacks and massacres – by the Zionist militias and, from May 1948, by the Israeli army; they were never allowed to return. This page shows the four waves, the figures and where they come from — and the scholarly dispute, which has long ceased to be about whether people were expelled.

The key points first

What is still disputed — and what no longer is

The narrative that could not be sustained

For decades the official Israeli account was that the Palestinians had left voluntarily, called on by Arab leaders over the radio to make way for the armies. (Reproduced and examined by Childers 1961 and Morris 2004, see below; no single government document containing this formula is cited here.)
Check: refuted This claim has been examined — and it has been refuted. In 1961 the Irish journalist Erskine Childers analysed the transcripts of the BBC Monitoring Service, which in 1948 had recorded and transcribed all radio broadcasts in the region. His finding:

“There was not a single order, or appeal, or suggestion about evacuation from Palestine from any Arab radio station, inside or outside Palestine, in 1948.”

Erskine B. Childers, “The Other Exodus”, The Spectator, 12 May 1961

Childers found the opposite: repeatedly recorded Arab appeals, in some cases explicit instructions to the civilian population to stay (issue in the Spectator archive).

What remains of it: In 1948 local Arab commanders and committees ordered the evacuation of individual localities, often only of women and children; Benny Morris documents this from the archives (The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem Revisited, Cambridge 2004). He does not document a general call by Arab governments over the radio to flee – and most localities were emptied by attacks, fear of massacres and expulsions, not in response to a call.

What is still disputed today

The scholarly dispute is no longer about whether people were expelled — Israeli military archives themselves document that. It is about the question of premeditation:

  • Benny Morris: The flight had several causes — fighting, panic after massacres, the collapse of order and targeted expulsions by Israeli units. He does not see a central expulsion plan.
  • Ilan Pappé: It was a deliberate ethnic cleansing, for which Plan Dalet provided the framework.

Both rely on the same Israeli archives. They disagree about intent, not about the facts. The debate in detail ↓

December 1947 to 1949

The four waves

The Nakba was not a single day. It unfolded in phases that differ in cause and composition — and the decisive one began before the Arab armies entered.

from Dec. 1947

The first wave: the city dwellers. After the partition resolution, a civil war breaks out between Jewish and Arab-Palestinian militias. The first to leave the cities are wealthier families — many assuming they would be gone for a few weeks.

April–May 1948

The decisive wave. With the large-scale offensives of the Haganah, Haifa, Jaffa, Tiberias and Safed fall, along with hundreds of villages. These weeks also saw the massacre of Deir Yassin (9 April 1948), news of which considerably accelerated the flight. This wave came before 15 May — that is, before the Arab armies entered. → Chronicle of massacres

Summer 1948

Lydda and Ramle. In July the inhabitants of both towns are forced to leave — the best-known individual case of an ordered expulsion, documented from Israeli military records.

Autumn 1948 – 1949

Galilee and the Negev. Further waves in Upper Galilee and in the south, some of them after the armistices. Even villages that had not fought or had concluded non-aggression agreements are cleared — among them the Christian villages of Iqrit and Bir'im, whose inhabitants won before Israeli courts and were nevertheless never allowed to return.

March 1948

Plan Dalet and the historians’ debate

No document from these months is quoted as often and as contradictorily as Plan Dalet (Plan D), adopted by the Haganah in March 1948.

Its content is undisputed: the plan expressly permitted the destruction of villages and the expulsion of their inhabitants in contested areas. What is disputed is how it should be interpreted.

PositionReading
Ilan Pappé
The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine (2006)		 Plan Dalet was part of a deliberate ethnic cleansing — the plan implemented what was politically intended.
Benny Morris
The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem (1987/2004)		 A military plan for the expected war, not a political expulsion order — but one that in its implementation led to expulsions, which Morris himself documents from the archives.
Walid Khalidi (as early as 1961; reprint: Journal of Palestine Studies 18/1, 1988, pp. 4–33) A “master plan for the conquest of Palestine”.
Avi Shlaim Represents the critical reassessment of the Israeli founding narrative, without fully adopting Pappé’s thesis of intentionality.

Check: disputed – state of research A genuine scholarly dispute about intent, not about the facts.

Documented and undisputed

Documented and undisputed are the wording of the plan (the text with the operational orders to the brigades is published in the appendix to Khalidi’s 1988 reprint), the expulsion orders in individual cases – for instance for Lydda and Ramle in July 1948 – and the subsequent decision to prevent return (Morris 2004).

Still open

What remains open is whether a central expulsion plan lay behind this (Pappé, Khalidi) or an interplay of military planning, local orders, massacres and panic (Morris).

Most scholars today take the second reading, without denying the deliberate expulsions.

Why this debate is set out in full here

Because it is conducted within Israeli and international historical scholarship — and because its outcome is unambiguous on one point: none of those named denies that people were expelled. Anyone who wants to deny the Nakba therefore has to contend not with Palestinian sources, but with Israeli military archives.

1950 to 1952

How return was made legally impossible

The expulsion did not end with the war. In the years that followed it was cast in legal form — and this part is the best documented, because it is written in the official gazettes.

YearLawEffect
1950Law of Return Every Jew worldwide is entitled to immigration and citizenship — even without ever having been in the country
1950Absentees' Property Law Transfers the property of those who fled or were expelled in 1948 to state administration — including that of the “present absentees”, i.e. those who never left the country, only their place of residence
The contrast in one sentence

In the same year, 1950, every Jew worldwide received a right to immigrate — and every expelled Palestinian lost his property to the state.

Both laws remain in force to this day. The right of return of the expelled under Resolution 194 has never been implemented.

Wording and further laws: Laws & ethnocracy (in German)

The term “present absentees” refers to a particularly revealing group: people who remained within Israel’s state territory but had left their place of residence during the war. They were present enough for citizenship — and absent enough to lose their property.

June 1967

The Naksa: the second expulsion

Nineteen years later the process repeated itself — for a considerable proportion of those affected for the second time.

≈ 300,000expelled from the West Bank and Gaza (estimates: 280,000–325,000)
≈ 145,000of them had already become refugees in 1948 (BADIL)
≈ 100,000Syrians additionally lost their homes on the Golan

Naksa means “setback”. The term stands for the events of the Six-Day War of 5 to 10 June 1967, in which Israel conquered Sinai, the Gaza Strip, the West Bank including East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights. Most of those expelled were denied return.

On 22 November 1967 the UN Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 242, which emphasises the “inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war” and calls for withdrawal from occupied territories. → What Resolution 242 says exactly · → The Six-Day War in detail

Two cases that show: this was not flight from fighting

Both clearances took place after the conquest, in territory already under control — and they are documented in Israeli as well as international sources:

  • The Latrun villages. In June 1967 the Israeli army destroyed Imwas, Yalu and Beit Nuba west of Jerusalem. The roughly 10,000 inhabitants were expelled and were never allowed to return. On part of the site the “Canada Park” was later created.
  • The Mughrabi Quarter in Jerusalem. In the night of 10 to 11 June 1967 Israeli authorities had the centuries-old Moroccan Quarter directly in front of the Western Wall demolished to create today’s plaza: around 135 houses as well as mosques and pilgrim hospices. About 650 to 700 people (around 108 families) were made homeless without any legal proceedings.

Here the question of “flight or expulsion” cannot even be asked: clearance and demolition took place when the fighting was over. → Further details
