A Hundred Years of War on a People: 1917 to 2026 in a Single Arc

This website has more than 70 pages. This one tells what holds them together: the history of a people that, since the Balfour Declaration of 1917, has been dispossessed, expelled, placed under foreign law and fought against anew in every generation — by the Mandate power, by the State of Israel and at times also by its Arab neighbours. Not as a “conflict” between two equal sides, but as what the historian Rashid Khalidi calls the hundred years’ war on Palestine: a succession of declarations of war on a population that wanted to remain on its land. Each stage has its own page on this website; here is the arc — with the figures that underpin it all.

The essentials first

The findings in four sentences

What a hundred years show

First: In 1917 around 700,000 people lived in Palestine, nine tenths of them Palestinians who owned and farmed the land. In 2026 there are around 14 million Palestinians — around half of them outside Palestine, 5.9 million of them registered refugees — and no Palestinian between the river and the sea lives under a government that he has chosen and that grants him equal rights. Second: The loss did not happen in a single event but in six stages, each with its own means: immigration and land purchases under British protection, expulsion in 1948, military law and expropriation, occupation in 1967, fragmentation through Oslo, siege and war against Gaza. Third: The means changed, but five things stayed the same: the land is taken, the law is unequal, the violence goes unpunished, the language conceals it — and those affected are declared the guilty party. Fourth: None of this was fate. At every stage there were alternatives that were rejected — from the two-state recommendation of 1947 to the Arab Peace Initiative of 2002 — and at every stage there was resistance that was criminalised as terror.

How to read this page

It is the starting point: those who know nothing read it first and then follow the reading path into the individual pages. Those who know the individual pages will find the connections here. All figures come from the pages of this website and carry their source and reference date there; here they are only brought together. The framework — “hundred years’ war”, “settler colonialism” — is a scholarly classification (Khalidi 2020, Wolfe 2006, Veracini 2010, Sa'di/Abu-Lughod 2007), not a slogan; the evidence for it is given below.

The genocide question – the UN finding

In September 2025 the UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that Israeli authorities and security forces in the Gaza Strip have committed and are continuing to commit genocide: four of the five acts of the Genocide Convention, with the intent to destroy “the only reasonable inference”. The arc

Six stages, six means — one direction

Rashid Khalidi counts six “declarations of war” on the Palestinians: 1917–39, 1947–48, 1967, 1982, 1987–95, 2000–2014. This page follows his division and continues it up to 2026. Each stage: What happened — by what means — what was lost — where it is covered on this website.

11917–1939 · The Mandate: promises to others, registers against the inhabitants In 1917 Britain promises a “national home” for the Jewish people in a country whose population is around 90% Arab, and from 1920 governs it with this mission. Means: immigration under the protection of the Mandate power (Jewish share of the population from 11% in 1922 to around 30% in 1939), land purchases from absentee large landowners, eviction of the tenant farmers, exclusion of Palestinian self-government. Resistance and response: The revolt of 1936–39 is crushed with around 5,000 Palestinians killed, mass arrests, the blowing up of houses and the destruction of the leadership — Israel takes over these methods (collective punishment, administrative detention, house demolition) in 1948 from British emergency law and applies them to this day. Loss: the capacity for political action before the decisive decade. → Mandate · → Massacres of the Mandate period · → Basel 1897
21947–1949 · The Nakba: the land is taken In 1947 the UN recommends partition: 55% of the land for the Jewish third of the population, which owns around 7% of the land. In the civil war and the war of 1948, around 750,000 people flee or are expelled — Plan Dalet, massacres (Deir Yassin, Tantura, Lydda, Dawayima), expulsion orders; more than 400 localities are destroyed. Israel is established on 78% of the Mandate territory. Means afterwards: a ban on return enforced by orders to shoot, the Absentees’ Property Law of 1950 (the property of the expelled passes to the state), military administration over those who remained, 1948–66. Loss: homeland, property, state, cohesion — the Nakba is the origin of everything that followed. → The Nakba · → Massacres of 1948 · → The documents · → The camps
31967 · The occupation: the rest of the land In the June War Israel occupies the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, the Golan and Sinai; around 300,000 people flee (Naksa), many for the second time. Means: military law for Palestinians, civil law for settlers (today more than 750,000, EU Representative 2026), East Jerusalem annexed, water, land and borders under Israeli control; collective punishment and administrative detention from the British legacy. Loss: the last 22% of the land; for 59 years not a single day of self-determination. In 2024 the ICJ declares the occupation unlawful. → West Bank · → Naksa · → Two legal systems
41970–1988 · The war against the exiles Those who have been expelled are fought where they arrive: Black September in Jordan in 1970 (Jordanian army), Tel al-Zaatar in 1976 (Lebanese militias), the invasion of Lebanon in 1982 with around 17,800 dead, and Sabra and Shatila (Phalange under an Israeli cordon), the expulsion of the PLO to Tunis, Hammam Chott in 1985, the War of the Camps 1985–88. Loss: the places of refuge; the realisation that no Arab state offers protection. → Palestine’s neighbouring states · → 1970–1982
51987–2005 · Uprising, Oslo, fragmentation The First Intifada (more than 1,000 dead) forces negotiations; Oslo 1993/95 brings an Authority without sovereignty and divides the West Bank into islands (Areas A and B) and 60% Area C under full Israeli control. The number of settlers doubles during the Oslo years. Camp David 2000: no written offer; the Second Intifada (2000–05, more than 3,000 Palestinians and more than 1,000 Israelis killed) ends with the barrier, checkpoints and permit regime — the administration of the occupation that is still in force today. Loss: the hope that negotiating would bring back land. → First Intifada · → Two-state solution · → Second Intifada · → Law in everyday life
62005–2026 · Siege, war, expulsion — and the neighbours In 2005 Gaza is “disengaged”, and from 2007 it is besieged; four wars 2008–2021; in 2018 the Nation-State Law. After 7 October 2023 comes the war that the ICJ is examining under the Genocide Convention in South Africa v. Israel: 74,618 killed (4 November 2026), 82% of buildings damaged, famine in August 2025, 90% of the population displaced; in the West Bank “Iron Wall” (more than 33,000 without return), Knesset votes on annexation in 2025, the death penalty law in 2026; in Lebanon 4,047 and 4,389 dead in two wars (Ministry of Health, 30 October 2026), in Iran two wars. At the same time: 154 to 157 of 193 states recognise Palestine (→ Recognition), ICC arrest warrants, the UN Commission of Inquiry found genocide in 2025 (→ finding) — the arc has not come to an end. → Gaza · → The genocide question · → Expulsion · → Neighbouring states · → Since 2025

The pattern

Five things that stayed the same for a hundred years

1 · The land is taken — always “legally”

British land ordinances, the Absentees’ Property Law of 1950, “state land” declarations after 1967, Area C, demolition orders, “nature reserves”, the cabinet package of 8 February 2026: every stage has its legal form. The result is the same. → Catalogue of laws

2 · One law for some, another for the others

The Emergency Regulations of 1945, military government 1948–66, military law since 1967 alongside civilian law for settlers, the Nation-State Law of 2018 — B’Tselem, HRW and Amnesty call it apartheid. → Two legal systems

3 · Violence goes unpunished

Deir Yassin, Kafr Qasim, Sabra and Shatila, Qana, the pagers, Hind Rajab, the settler attacks: in a hundred years, convictions remained the rare exception and were quickly mitigated: after Kafr Qasim (1956), eight border policemen were convicted in 1958 and were free again by the end of 1959; the brigade commander received a symbolic fine of ten prutot. Yesh Din: 93.6% of investigations into settler violence end without an indictment; of complaints against soldiers, fewer than one in a hundred leads to an indictment. → Impunity · → Outcome table

4 · Language conceals it

“A land without a people”, “left voluntarily”, “clashes”, “evacuation”, “disputed territories”, “human shields”: hasbara is as old as the project itself. → Hasbara: claims and evidence

5 · Those affected are made the guilty ones

Whoever resists is a terrorist; whoever counts is Hamas; whoever sues is an antisemite; whoever stays is a shield; whoever leaves left voluntarily. The reversal of perpetrator and victim is the constant behind all the others. → The term and its instrumentalisation

“The war against the Palestinians was not waged in one stroke but in stages — each declared by a different power, each aimed at ending their existence as a people on their land.” (translated from German)

Paraphrased from Rashid Khalidi, The Hundred Years' War on Palestine (2020) — the exact wording is in the book, introduction
The context

Settler colonialism: why the term fits — and what it does not claim

What the term says

In 2006 the historian Patrick Wolfe summed up settler colonialism in the formula “invasion is a structure not an event”: settlers come to stay, and the existing population is replaced through a “logic of elimination” — through expulsion, assimilation or enclosure. The Zionist founders themselves called their project colonisation (the Jewish Colonial Trust as the Zionist Organization’s bank, Herzl’s writings, Jabotinsky’s “Iron Wall” of 1923: the native population — in the original “the Arabs” — would never submit voluntarily, so force was needed). Research (Wolfe, Veracini, Sa'di/Abu-Lughod, Pappé, Khalidi, Yiftachel) has applied the term since the 2000s; it explains why the stages above belong together. → Zionism & the founding of the state

What it does not say

It does not say that Jews have no connection to the land, nor that antisemitism was not a reason for flight, nor that Israeli Jews today would have to “leave” — the research itself stresses that settler societies stay and that the solution lies in equal rights, not in reversal. And it replaces no evidence: every figure on this website stands on its own, independent of the framework. → The research · → The Jews in the land
