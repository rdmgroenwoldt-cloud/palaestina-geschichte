Displacement

The displacement of the Palestinians is usually told as a closed chapter of 1948. It has never come to a halt since — it has only changed its form: from military operation to demolition order. This section shows the line and the legal position.

🌐 Deutsch · Español

This page is a translation of the German original (as of 15 November 2026). Links lead to German pages unless marked otherwise.

The essentials first

Here, displacement is not an event but a condition

The finding of this page

The displacement of the Palestinians is usually told as one event: 1948, the Nakba, over and done with. That is wrong — and verifiably wrong. Since 1948, displacement has never come to a halt; it has only changed its form.

  • In 1948 it happened through war and military operations.
  • In 1967 through war and denied return.
  • Today it happens through administrative acts: demolition orders, denied building permits, military closed zones, revocation of residency — and through violence that goes unpunished until a community gives up of its own accord.

The result is the same: people lose their place and are not allowed to return. The only difference is that the second route produces no images that get reported on.

Why this is legally decisive

Displacement is not a political issue on which one can hold differing opinions. It is expressly prohibited under international law — in a sentence that deliberately leaves no back door: “regardless of their motive”. What exactly that means is set out in the next chapter.

≈ 750,000 displaced 1947–1949 — the Nakba
≈ 300,000 displaced in 1967 — the Naksa, around 145,000 of them for the second time
5.9 million refugees and descendants registered with UNRWA today
58 recognised refugee camps in five areas
The legal position

What international law says about displacement

Four provisions are relevant. They come from different decades and from different areas of law — and they all say the same thing.

1949Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention

The central sentence comes right in the first paragraph and is one of the few in international humanitarian law that manage without any balancing clause:

“Individual or mass forcible transfers, as well as deportations of protected persons from occupied territory to the territory of the Occupying Power or to that of any other country, occupied or not, are prohibited, regardless of their motive.”

Fourth Geneva Convention of 12 August 1949, Article 49, paragraph 1
Three things about this sentence that are easy to overlook
  • “Regardless of their motive”. There is no justification — not even one based on security policy. The sentence was deliberately worded this way in 1949, because in the Second World War deportations had routinely been justified on security grounds.
  • The displacement of a single individual is enough. It does not have to be a mass expulsion for a violation to exist.
  • According to the prevailing interpretation, it applies to displacement within the occupied territory just as much as to deportation out of it — this is how the ICRC and the international criminal tribunals read the prohibition of “forcible transfer”, even though the wording expressly names only removal to the outside.

The only exceptionEvacuation — and the duty to return

Paragraph 2 permits an exception, and this exception is the point on which everything in Gaza turns: an occupying power may evacuate an area in whole or in part if the security of the population or imperative military reasons so demand.

The condition that is almost always left out

The same paragraph stipulates: the evacuated persons are to be transferred back to their homes as soon as hostilities in the area in question have ceased.

An evacuation is therefore temporary by its very nature. If return is permanently prevented, it was no longer an evacuation but a displacement — and that is prohibited under paragraph 1. It is precisely this that decides the legal assessment, not the day of the clearance.

1998The Rome Statute: war crimes and crimes against humanity

ProvisionWhat it coversClassification
Art. 8(2)(b)(viii)The deportation or transfer of all or parts of the civilian population of an occupied territory within or outside that territory — and the transfer of the occupier’s own civilian population into the occupied territoryWar crime
Art. 7(1)(d)Deportation or forcible transfer of population when committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against the civilian population Crime against humanity

The difference is not academic: a war crime is the individual act; a crime against humanity requires a pattern. Anyone seeking to prove the latter must show that the individual cases are connected — which is why the documentation collected on this website is more than a list of cases.

1948Resolution 194: the right of return

On 11 December 1948, the UN General Assembly resolved that refugees who wish to live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to return at the earliest practicable date — and that those who do not wish to return should receive compensation. The resolution has been reaffirmed every year since.

Why all negotiations have failed on this point is explained on the page Two-state solution.

The objection — and what remains of it

Against the application of the Fourth Geneva Convention, it is argued that it does not apply to the West Bank and Gaza, because before 1967 these territories did not belong to any recognised state. This has been the position of the Israeli government since 1967, worked out by Meir Shamgar, later President of the Supreme Court (“The Observance of International Law in the Administered Territories”, Israel Yearbook on Human Rights 1, 1971, pp. 262–277).
Check: refuted Outside Israel, practically no one has accepted this view. The International Court of Justice expressly rejected it in its 2004 advisory opinion (Wall advisory opinion of 9 July 2004, paras. 90–101) and reaffirmed this in 2024; so did the UN Security Council, the General Assembly, the International Committee of the Red Cross and the States parties to the Convention. The Israeli Supreme Court, too, applies the humanitarian-law provisions of the law of occupation in its case law — in doing so it relies on the Hague Regulations and applies provisions of the Geneva Convention that it regards as customary law, without formally rejecting the government’s position.

The line of continuity

From 1948 to today — the same movement in four stages

This timeline does not contain a single figure that is not documented elsewhere on this website. It merely shows what becomes visible when the stages are placed side by side.

1947–1949

The Nakba. Around 700,000 to 750,000 people flee or are expelled; in 1949 the UN Conciliation Commission put the figure at 711,000. Between 418 and more than 530 localities are depopulated. Some 150,000 Palestinians remain in the new State of Israel. → The Nakba in detail

1950–1966

Entrenchment. Tent camps become concrete settlements. Israel passes laws that make return legally impossible and transfer the property left behind to the state. UNRWA, created in 1949 as a temporary solution, is still operating today. → The camps

1967

The Naksa. In the Six-Day War, 280,000 to 325,000 people flee or are expelled from the West Bank and Gaza — among them around 145,000 who had already become refugees in 1948 and now lose their homes for the second time. Most are denied return. → The Naksa

1967 to today

Administered displacement. Procedure takes the place of war: demolition orders, denied building permits, closed areas, revocation of residency. In the West Bank, the Bedouin communities are particularly affected; in Gaza, around 90 percent of the population have been displaced since October 2023, many of them multiple times. → West Bank · → Gaza

The sentence that matters

What was done with armies in 1948 is done today with official notices.

This is not an exaggeration but the sober description of an administrative process: anyone who cannot get a building permit builds without one — and is demolished for it. Anyone who lives in a closed zone lives there unlawfully, even if their family has been there for generations.

As of August 2026

What has been added since 2025

The four stages above do not end in 2024. This is the situation as of 14 August 2026 — with the figures of the bodies collecting them; the details are on the two sub-pages.

Gaza Strip

4 February 2025: US President Trump declares that the US would take over Gaza (“The U.S. will take over the Gaza Strip”) and permanently resettle the population in neighbouring countries — an open announcement of displacement by the government of a major power; Egypt and Jordan refuse. 10 October 2025: ceasefire; the Israeli army withdraws to the “yellow line” and continues to control around half of the Strip. 16 June 2026 (OCHA/UNOSAT): 201,290 buildings damaged — 82% of all structures —, of which 134,422 destroyed; over 60 million t of rubble, of which around 630,000 t cleared. Return: between February and April 2026, 1,224 people returned via Rafah (Gisha). 23 June 2026: The UN Commission of Inquiry finds that Israeli forces deliberately targeted Palestinian children and assesses the conduct as genocide. Reconstruction: “Board of Peace” (since 18 February 2026, 17 billion dollars pledged), ISF with Rafah as first area of operations — not deployed as of August 2026. → in detail

West Bank

Since January 2025 (“Iron Wall”): the Jenin, Tulkarem and Nur Shams camps cleared, over 40,000 displaced, more than 33,000 unable to return as of August 2026 (OCHA). Demolitions have been running since 2024 at least twice as high every month as the 15-year average (OCHA, 8/2026). Annexation steps: Knesset declaration in favour of “sovereignty” 23 July 2025 (71:13); preliminary reading of two annexation bills 22 October 2025 (25:24 and 32:9, frozen thereafter); approval of 19 settlements 11 December 2025; security cabinet 8 February 2026: easier land acquisition for Israelis; law for an Israeli antiquities authority in the West Bank (3 February 2026). Toll for 2025 (OCHA): 227 Palestinians killed, 1,680 settler attacks — in October 2025, at 264, the highest monthly figure since counting began in 2006. Response: sanctions by the EU (28 May 2026) and by six states (9 June 2026) against settler networks. → in detail
