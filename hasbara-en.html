Hasbara: the claims — and what the evidence says

Hasbara (Hebrew for “explanation”) is the Israeli term for state-organised public diplomacy — an apparatus of ministries, agencies, language guides and, since 2024, fake accounts. This page takes each claim individually that this apparatus has repeated for a hundred years — from the “land without a people” to the “command centre under the hospital” — and sets against it what courts, UN investigations, Israeli historians, human rights organisations and government records themselves say. It also names the cases in which the Israeli state demonstrably made false accusations — and how they ended. Honesty applies in both directions: where the Palestinian side had to correct itself is documented here as well.

What this page finds

Three things to know about hasbara

First: it is an apparatus, not an opinion. A dedicated ministry (Strategic Affairs, later Diaspora Affairs), budgets in the millions, language guides for politicians and journalists (the 2009 Luntz handbook), advertising campaigns in Europe (2023), and in 2024, demonstrably, hundreds of fake accounts posing as Americans — shut down by OpenAI and Meta, paid for by the Diaspora Ministry with around two million dollars (New York Times). The 2009 language handbook is analysed in full on this page — and its most striking finding is an absence: in 116 pages, international law is not mentioned once. → Chapter 1 · → Chapter 8

Second: the claims have been the same for a hundred years. “A land without a people” (1901), “They left voluntarily” (1948), “We offered everything” (2000), “We left Gaza” (2005), “They use human shields” (every war). Each of them has been refuted or undermined by Israeli historians, the International Court of Justice or UN inquiries — the evidence is in chapters 2 to 4f, each with its evidence rating. → Chapter 2

Third: the state has repeatedly made demonstrably false claims. The “40 beheaded babies”, the “command centre under al-Shifa”, “UNRWA is Hamas”, “Hamas steals the aid”, “no troops near Hind Rajab” — each of these accusations was refuted by independent investigations or could never be substantiated. None has been retracted. → Chapter 5 · → further claims (27–46) · → law, history, ceasefire (47–66) · → from the Luntz handbook (67–71) · → recent campaigns (72–77)

How this page checks

Every claim gets three lines: what is claimed — what the evidence says (with author, document, date) — evidence rating: refuted (counter-evidence exists), discredited (claim without evidence, counter-indications prevail), oversimplified (true core, but used in a misleading way), open. Where a claim has a true core, it is stated — otherwise the page itself would be hasbara. And Chapter 6 lists the cases in which the Palestinian side was wrong.

The apparatus: ministries, language guides, fake accounts

1The term and the authorities Hasbara literally means “explaining” and since the 1970s has referred to the state’s presentation of itself abroad. In 2006 the Ministry of Strategic Affairs was created; from 2015 it worked mainly against the BDS movement. In 2021 its tasks passed to the Foreign Ministry, and from 2023 the Ministry of Diaspora Affairs and Combating Antisemitism ran its own campaigns. Alongside them: the IDF Spokesperson’s Unit, the National Information Directorate and a network of semi-governmental organisations (StandWithUs, the “Act.IL” app 2017–2022, Hasbara Fellowships).
22009 · The language handbook Written for “The Israel Project” by the US pollster Frank Luntz, the “Global Language Dictionary” (April 2009, marked “Not for distribution or publication” and leaked to Newsweek) sets out over 116 pages how to talk: 25 rules, plus chapters on settlements, the barrier, the right of return, the UN and target audiences — always contrasted as “Words That Work” and “Words That Don't Work”. Many of the claims below appear there as recommendations. This page has worked through the document in full: → Chapter 8, rule by rule
3Autumn 2023 · Advertising in Europe: 7.1 million dollars in one month After 7 October, the Israeli Foreign Ministry placed ads with images of the Hamas atrocities on YouTube and X: in October 2023 alone, around 7.1 million dollars on YouTube ads, mostly targeted at Europe — almost 50 video ads for EU countries, millions of impressions (Politico, analysis of Google’s transparency data); in France, ads depicting children were removed after complaints.
42025/2026 · The budget is quadrupled For 2025 the government granted the Foreign Ministry 545 million shekels (around 150 million dollars) for “public diplomacy”; in the 2026 budget this became 2.35 billion shekels — around 730 million dollars, more than four times as much (Times of Israel, 2025/26). The study “The Eighth Front” by the Quincy Institute puts the entire influence campaign, including private actors, at around one billion dollars. Israel Hayom (14 August 2025) in its headline on the previous budget: “failed catastrophically” — approval ratings fell regardless.
52024 · The fake accounts On 30 May 2024, OpenAI announced that it had shut down a network run by the Tel Aviv firm STOIC that used AI-generated comments to spread pro-Israeli content on Facebook, Instagram, X, YouTube and Telegram; Meta removed the same operation. On 5 June 2024 the New York Times documented that the client was the Diaspora Ministry, with a budget of around two million dollars; at its peak, hundreds of accounts posed as Americans and specifically urged Black Democratic lawmakers such as Hakeem Jeffries and Raphael Warnock to keep funding Israel’s military. The ministry denied involvement; the evidence (contracts, tender) went uncontested. Evidence rating: documented.
62019–2021 · The intelligence service against the court According to reporting by the Guardian (28 May 2024), Mossad chief Yossi Cohen waged a years-long campaign to dissuade ICC prosecutor Fatou Bensouda from pursuing an investigation: unannounced meetings, phone calls, threats against her and her family (“Did you forget what I do for a living?”), as well as surveillance of the court. Bensouda opened the investigation in 2021 regardless; the arrest warrants followed in 2024. Evidence rating: documented (three sources; an ICC spokesperson confirmed “several forms of threats and communications that could be viewed as attempts to unduly influence its activities”).

Chapter 2 · Claims about history

“A land without a people” and seven other founding myths

1 · “Palestine was a land without a people for a people without a land.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: The British census of 1922 counted 757,182 inhabitants, of whom around 78% were Muslims, 11% Jews and 10% Christians; in 1931 the figure was 1,036,000. The country exported Jaffa oranges, grain and soap from Nablus; it had towns, newspapers (Falastin from 1911) and schools. The phrase comes from 19th-century Christian Zionism and was popularised in 1901 by Israel Zangwill — Zionists themselves knew better: after his journey in 1891, Ahad Ha'am wrote that the land was “not empty” (translated from German). Genetics confirms this from another direction: ancient DNA shows continuous settlement by the same population since the Bronze Age — the land always had a people. Read more: Origins: what genetics shows · Ottoman Palestine · The Jews in the land.

2 · “There never was a Palestinian people — they are Arabs who could just as well live elsewhere.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: The name Palestine is attested since antiquity (Herodotus, the Roman province of Syria Palaestina, the Arab Jund Filastin); the Mandate was officially called Palestine, and from 1925 its inhabitants had Palestinian citizenship with a passport. Whether a national identity emerged “only” in the 20th century is irrelevant to its reality — the same applies to all modern nations, including the Israeli one. Rashid Khalidi (Palestinian Identity, 1997) documents its formation from the late 19th century onwards. And: the phrase “they could live elsewhere” is the justification for every expulsion. “Arab” describes language and culture; according to ancient DNA, the ancestors of the Palestinians were already living in the land in the Bronze Age. Read more: The “Arab” label · Eras · People.

3 · “The Palestinians left voluntarily in 1948 — on the orders of Arab radio stations.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: In 1961 the Irish journalist Erskine Childers examined the complete BBC and CIA monitoring records of all Arab stations from 1948 — there were no calls to evacuate, but there were calls to stay. Using the army archives, the Israeli historian Benny Morris showed that the majority fled because of attacks, expulsion orders and massacres (Lydda/Ramla: an explicit order); Plan Dalet of March 1948 provided for the clearing of villages. More than 400 villages were subsequently destroyed, and return was prevented by law. The true core: part of the upper class left the towns early; that does not change the character of the expulsion. Read more: Plan Dalet · Massacres of 1948.

4 · “Israel accepted partition; the Arabs rejected it and attacked.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The true core: the Arab states and the Palestinian leadership rejected Resolution 181. The part left unsaid: the plan allotted 55% of the land to the Jewish third of the population, which owned around 7% of the land; the expulsion of around 300,000 Palestinians (Haifa, Jaffa, Tiberias, Safed) began before the Arab armies entered on 15 May 1948; Ben-Gurion’s diaries show that the leadership regarded the partition borders as a stage. Read more: Zionism & the founding of the state · The four waves.

5 · “850,000 Jews were expelled from Arab countries — a population exchange.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The expulsion and flight of Jews from Arab countries is real and is covered on this website with perpetrators and figures. But: it took place over two decades, from two dozen states, in part through recruitment by the Israeli state itself; the Palestinians caused none of it — they do not owe the debt for crimes committed by Baghdad or Cairo. An “exchange” presupposes that two sides exchange something; here one people lost its land for the crimes of others. Read more: Jewish immigration — and who expelled the Jews of the Arab world.

6 · “The Palestinians rejected every peace offer — in 2000 Israel offered almost everything.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: At Camp David in 2000 there was no written offer; half a year later both sides accepted the Clinton Parameters with reservations; Israel broke off Taba 2001 because of the election date. Robert Malley, Clinton’s adviser, publicly contradicted the “Arafat said no” version in 2001. Israel never responded to the Arab Peace Initiative of 2002 — full normalisation in exchange for the 1967 borders; according to US mediators, the Kerry process of 2014 was wrecked by the settlements. No offer ever provided for the right of return or the whole of the West Bank. Read more: The two-state solution — why has it never worked?

7 · “Israel made the desert bloom.”Evidence rating: discredited

What the evidence says: In 1938 Palestine exported around 15 million cases of citrus fruit, most of it from Arab-owned groves; the hill country had been terraced and planted with olives for millennia (Canaan, the “land flowing with milk and honey”). After 1948 Israel took over tens of thousands of hectares of cultivated fields, olive groves and orange plantations belonging to those expelled. The phrase turns dispossession into land reclamation. Read more: The land was never empty · Who owned the land? · The olive tree.

8 · “Jews are the indigenous people; Palestinians are Arab conquerors from the 7th century.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: The population of Palestine was never replaced — over the centuries it changed language and religion (Canaanite, Aramaic, Greek, Arabic; Jewish, Christian, Muslim). Archaeological research finds no evidence of an “expulsion of the Jews” by Rome; Christianisation and later Islamisation happened mainly through conversion. Demographically, today’s Palestinians are the descendants of the ancient population — as ancient DNA now also shows: continuity since the Bronze Age, supplemented by a smaller influx from Arabia and Africa, no replacement. As early as 1917, David Ben-Gurion wrote that the Palestinian peasants were largely descended from the ancient Jewish rural population. And even if it were otherwise: descent from 2,000 years ago gives no right to expel people living today. Read more: The Jews in the land — the expulsion narrative put to the test · Origins of the Palestinians: what genetics shows.

Chapter 3 · Claims about occupation and law

“There is no occupation” and six other legal claims

9 · “There is no occupation — the West Bank is ‘disputed territory’.”Evidence rating: refuted (ICJ 2004 and 2024)

What the evidence says: In 2004 (the Wall advisory opinion) and on 19 July 2024, the International Court of Justice found that the West Bank including East Jerusalem and the Gaza Strip are occupied territory, that the occupation is unlawful and must be ended as rapidly as possible, and that the settlements violate Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention. The Security Council (Res. 2334, 2016) and the General Assembly (Sept. 2024) say the same; Israel’s own Supreme Court has also applied the law of occupation since 1967. Apart from Israel, no court in the world calls it “disputed”. Read more: ICJ advisory opinions · West Bank.

10 · “Israel left Gaza completely in 2005 — Gaza is not occupied.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: In 2005 Israel evacuated the settlements but kept control of the airspace, the coast, all crossings except Rafah, the population registry, electricity, the fishing zone and a buffer zone inside the Strip itself; since 2007 there has been the blockade with its “calorie calculation”. That is why the UN, the ICRC, the ICJ (2024) and Israel’s own legal adviser in 2005 continue to classify Gaza as occupied or under effective control. Read more: The “disengagement” — and what to make of it.

11 · “Apartheid? Arabs vote and sit in the Knesset and on the Supreme Court.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The true core: Palestinian citizens of Israel have the right to vote. The apartheid finding of B'Tselem (2021), Human Rights Watch (2021) and Amnesty (2022) refers to the overall system between the river and the sea: seven million Jews and seven million Palestinians under a single rule — the former with full rights, the latter, depending on where they live, as second-class citizens (Nation-State Law 2018), as residents without citizenship (East Jerusalem), under military law (West Bank) or under blockade (Gaza). Two legal systems on the same land — that is the legal definition, not the voting rights of one subgroup. Read more: The apartheid question · Two legal systems.

12 · “Israel is the only democracy in the Middle East.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: For its Jewish citizens, Israel has democratic institutions. But a state that has ruled more than five million people under military law without the right to vote for 59 years is no democracy for those people; the political geographer Oren Yiftachel calls the system an ethnocracy. The 2018 Nation-State Law stipulates that the right to self-determination is “unique to the Jewish people”. Read more: Ethnocracy · Nation-State Law.

13 · “Egypt blockades Gaza just the same.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: Egypt does indeed keep Rafah mostly closed and bears responsibility for that — this website says so explicitly. But Rafah is a crossing for people, not a cargo port; goods, electricity, water, airspace, the coast, the population registry and, since May 2024, the Gaza side of Rafah as well are controlled by Israel. A country that keeps six of seven doors shut is not innocent just because another one closes the seventh. Read more: Egypt: the second bolt · Egypt.

14 · “Criticism of Israel is antisemitism.”Evidence rating: refuted (including by the IHRA definition itself)

What the evidence says: Even the IHRA working definition states: “criticism of Israel similar to that leveled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic.” And its lead author Kenneth Stern, himself Jewish, warns against enshrining it in laws and government rules, because it would then suppress criticism of Israel (→ Stern, → criticism of the IHRA definition). The Jerusalem Declaration (2021, more than 200 scholars of antisemitism) makes clear that boycott, apartheid analysis and criticism of Zionism as such are not antisemitic. Today the accusation is directed above all at Jewish critics (Jewish Voice for Peace, Breaking the Silence, Israeli historians) and at Palestinians who speak about their own expulsion. Read more: Antisemitism — the term and its instrumentalisation.

15 · “Palestinian schoolbooks teach hatred.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The EU-commissioned study by the Georg Eckert Institute (2021) of 156 Palestinian schoolbooks found that they largely meet UNESCO standards, alongside some problematic passages and anti-Jewish content in a few places — which must be criticised. At the same time, studies of Israeli schoolbooks (Nurit Peled-Elhanan, 2012) show Palestinians almost exclusively as a threat or not at all. Anyone who explains children in Gaza with “schoolbooks” conceals the fact that since 2023 not a single school has been left standing there. Read more: Scholasticide.

Chapter 4 · Claims about Israel’s assault on Gaza since 2023

“Human shields” and ten more claims

16 · “Hamas uses civilians as human shields — that is why so many die.”Evidence rating: oversimplified — and documented in reverse

What the evidence says: That Hamas operates from residential areas is documented and unlawful — but it does not lift the attacker’s duty of distinction and proportionality (Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions, Art. 51(8)). What the claim leaves out: Israel’s army itself uses Palestinians as human shields — documented by Breaking the Silence, Haaretz, AP, CBS and the UN Commission: the “mosquito protocol”, in which detainees (“shawish”) in uniform are sent ahead of the soldiers into tunnels and houses. A senior officer wrote anonymously in Haaretz on 30 March 2025: “almost every platoon keeps a ‘shawish,’ and no infantry force enters a house before a ‘shawish’ clears it”; human shields are used “at least six times a day”; in May 2025 a soldier confirmed this to CBS. Israel’s Supreme Court had already banned the practice (the “neighbor procedure”) in 2005. Read more: Human shields.

17 · “There is a Hamas command center under every hospital.”Evidence rating: discredited

What the evidence says: For al-Shifa, Gaza’s largest hospital, the army announced a “command center” with a 3D animation in October 2023. After storming it in November, it showed a tunnel, some weapons and laptops; an analysis by the Washington Post (21 December 2023) found that the evidence presented did not support the claim of a command center. The WHO documented that between 2023 and 2025 almost every hospital in Gaza was attacked or put out of service — Kamal Adwan, al-Ahli, Nasser, the European Hospital. A suspicion does not justify the destruction of a health system. Read more: Hospitals.

18 · “The casualty figures come from Hamas and are made up.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: The Ministry of Health in Gaza publishes lists of names with ID numbers (72,835 names are searchable on this website); its figures in the wars of 2008, 2014 and 2021 were later found to be accurate (UN, Israeli army). The journal The Lancet estimated in January 2025 that the number of people killed by injuries up to June 2024 was 64,260 — about 41% higher than the official list, so the undercount runs in the other direction. According to Haaretz, the Israeli army internally accepts the order of magnitude. Two Israelis from the spokesperson’s unit have never presented a count of their own. Read more: How many people have died · The names.

19 · “There is no famine — Israel lets in more aid than ever before.”Evidence rating: refuted (IPC, August 2025)

What the evidence says: The IPC — the global standard backed by FAO, WFP and UNICEF — formally confirmed famine in Gaza City on 22 August 2025; the same day Israel’s Foreign Ministry called it “fabricated” and COGAT “false and biased”, both without data of their own. From March to May 2025 Israel had allowed in no aid for eleven weeks, then replaced UN distribution with the four GHF distribution sites, where hundreds of aid seekers were shot. Chapter 4d · More claims about the law

“The wall is a security fence” and six more

47 · “The barrier is a security fence — it stopped the terror.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: No court has denied Israel the right to protect itself — what is objected to is the route. On 9 July 2004 the International Court of Justice found that, insofar as the barrier stands on occupied territory, it is contrary to international law; Israel must dismantle the sections concerned, repeal the permit regime and make reparation for damage. The General Assembly demanded compliance on 20 July 2004 by 150 votes to 6. Around 85% of the route does not follow the Green Line but instead encloses settlements and cuts villages off from their fields. Israel’s Supreme Court also ordered re-routing (Beit Surik 2004, Bil'in 2007) — not because security was impermissible, but because this route was disproportionate. And the barrier is still not complete today: the decline in attacks after 2005 also occurred where nothing had been built. Read more: The Seam Zone · The system of control.

48 · “The settlements are lawful — an Israeli government report says so itself.”Evidence rating: refuted

What the evidence says: Article 49, paragraph 6 of the Fourth Geneva Convention prohibits the occupying power from transferring parts of its own civilian population into the occupied territory. As early as September 1967, the legal adviser to the Israeli Foreign Ministry, Theodor Meron, reached exactly this conclusion in an internal opinion for the government — it remained unpublished for decades. The Security Council reaffirmed it in Resolution 446 (1979) and 2334 of 23 December 2016 (14–0 with the United States abstaining: settlements have no legal validity, a “flagrant violation”), as did the International Court of Justice in 2004 and again on 19 July 2024. The Levy Report (July 2012), on which the claim relies, came from a committee appointed by the government itself, was never formally adopted as a government position and found no international acceptance. Read more: Article 49 · Two legal systems.

49 · “Israel is acting in self-defence — Article 51 of the UN Charter covers it.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: In 2004 the International Court of Justice expressly addressed this argument (Advisory Opinion, paragraph 139): Article 51 presupposes an armed attack by a state and does not apply to threats originating from a territory that the occupying power itself controls. Above all, however: self-defence is a right to go to war, not a licence for anything in war. Distinction between combatants and civilians, proportionality and precautions (Articles 48, 51 and 57 of Additional Protocol I) bind both sides regardless of who started it — otherwise every war crime could be justified by pointing to the enemy. Read more: The legal framework · The genocide question.

50 · “The blockade is lawful — a UN report confirmed it.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: This refers to the report of the Panel of Inquiry set up by the UN Secretary-General under Geoffrey Palmer (September 2011) on the 2010 flotilla. It held the naval blockade to be lawful — but it was not a court ruling; it was an inquiry report by two government representatives and two jurists, and at the same time it criticised the “excessive and unreasonable” force used in the boarding, which left nine dead. The parallel fact-finding mission of the UN Human Rights Council (September 2010) reached the opposite conclusion. The International Committee of the Red Cross stated on 14 June 2010 that the closure constituted collective punishment and thus violated Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention. And: the blockade covers land, air and sea — only part of it was assessed. Read more: The siege · The aid balance sheet.

51 · “The International Court of Justice has not found genocide — so there is none.”Evidence rating: oversimplified — the case is still pending

What the evidence says: The Court has not yet ruled on the merits at all — no judgment is not an acquittal. So far there are three orders on provisional measures (26 January 2024: the rights of Palestinians under the Genocide Convention are “plausible”; 28 March 2024: humanitarian aid; 24 May 2024: Rafah). On the merits, Israel filed its Counter-Memorial in March 2026, in which it challenged the jurisdiction of the Court; by an order of 21 May 2026 the Court set South Africa a time-limit of 22 November 2027 for its Reply and Israel a time-limit of 22 May 2029 for its Rejoinder. A judgment is therefore not to be expected before the end of the decade at the earliest. What exists alongside this: the UN Commission of Inquiry (September 2025, expanded in 2026), Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch (both December 2024) — and experts who disagree. Both are set out, with names and reasoning, on the genocide page. Read more: The genocide question · UN documents.

52 · “Calls for boycotts are antisemitic — and banned in Europe anyway.”Evidence rating: refuted (legally)

What the evidence says: On 11 June 2020, the European Court of Human Rights ruled unanimously (Baldassi and Others v. France, nos. 15271/16 and others) that the conviction of activists for calling for a boycott of Israeli goods violated Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights: such calls are political expression on a matter of public interest — the line lies at calls for violence, hatred or intolerance. The Bundestag resolution of 17 May 2019 (a motion of the German parliament) is not a law and is not legally binding. What remains true: where a boycott is directed not against a state’s policies but against people because they are Jews, it is hatred of Jews — and must be called that. The difference can be tested, and that is exactly why the campaign flattens it. Read more: The criminalisation of resistance · Antisemitism.

53 · “Administrative detention is a normal security tool that many states use.”Evidence rating: refuted in this form

What the evidence says: The Fourth Geneva Convention permits internment (Article 78) only exceptionally, for imperative reasons of security and on a case-by-case basis — not as a permanent instrument against a population. Chapter 4e · More claims about history

“The land was bought” and four more

54 · “The land was bought, not taken.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: There were purchases — they just do not explain the outcome. According to the British Survey of Palestine, at the end of 1945 around 6% of the land was in Jewish ownership (research gives 6–7% depending on the method of counting); in 1949 Israel controlled around 78% of the Mandate territory. That difference did not come from purchase contracts but from flight, expulsion and the Absentees’ Property Law of 1950. Then there is the question of who sold: mostly absentee landowners, such as the Sursuq family in Beirut. The tenant farmers who had lived on the land for generations lost their livelihoods in the process — the British inquiries by Shaw and Hope Simpson (both 1930) recorded exactly this and therefore recommended protective rules. What the evidence says: Plan Dalet (March 1948) provided for the capture of Arab localities and, where resistance was offered, their destruction and the expulsion of their inhabitants. In 2004 the Israeli historian Benny Morris documented expulsion orders and at least 24 massacres from Israeli military archives; 418 villages were depopulated and largely razed according to Walid Khalidi (“All That Remains”), more than 530 localities according to Salman Abu Sitta (→ why the figures differ). Afterwards, return was prevented by shoot-to-kill orders along the armistice lines and, from 1950, by the Absentees’ Property Law. That people also fled the fighting is undisputed — the two are not mutually exclusive, and the difference is decided not by the leaving but by not being allowed back. On the alleged radio appeals: Claim 3. Read more: Plan Dalet · The places.

56 · “Zionism was not colonialism — Jews were returning to their homeland.”Evidence rating: refuted by the movement’s own sources

What the evidence says: Initially the movement called itself that: the bank founded in 1899 was named the “Jewish Colonial Trust”, the settlement company the “Jewish Colonization Association”. In 1923, in “The Iron Wall”, Ze'ev Jabotinsky wrote explicitly that any colonisation against the will of the native population needed a military wall behind which it could proceed. What is not disputed here: the refuge from persecution was real, and the bond to the land is thousands of years old. The term describes not the motives of the immigrants but their relationship to the population that was already there — what the historian Patrick Wolfe calls settler colonialism: a structure, not an event. 57 · “The 1988 Hamas Charter shows what the Palestinians want.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The 1988 Charter contains openly anti-Jewish passages, including an appeal to the forged “Protocols of the Elders of Zion” — this is stated here without softening. 58 · “The Palestinians rejected a state five times — 1937, 1947, 1967, 2000, 2008.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: Each of the five dates stands for something other than a rejected offer of statehood. In 1947 the partition plan awarded 56% of the land to a third of the population, which owned 6–7% of the soil. In 1967 there was no offer on the table, but a new occupation. Chapter 4g · The points that were in the handbook and were still missing here

“The settlements protect Israel” and four more

After the complete analysis of the Luntz handbook (Chapter 8), every factual claim recommended there was checked against the 66 claims this page contained at the time. Most were already here. Five were not — they follow, examined according to the same scheme.

67 · “The settlements are there for security reasons — they are an early-warning system.”Evidence rating: refuted (including by Israeli security experts)

What the evidence says: Israeli military figures themselves contradict this. The association Commanders for Israel's Security — several hundred former generals, intelligence and police chiefs, since 2014 — considers the settlements in the West Bank a security burden: they permanently tie down troops that must protect them, and scatter forces across an area that could otherwise be secured along a line. Early warning today is provided by radar, reconnaissance and satellites, not by residential buildings. And legally the argument does not hold anyway: Article 49, paragraph 6 of the Fourth Geneva Convention prohibits the transfer of one’s own civilian population; military necessity can justify temporary military installations, not permanent towns with universities and industrial zones. On 19 July 2024, the International Court of Justice concluded that security considerations do not justify the settlement policy. In fact, the settlements are not located only at strategic points either: Area C covers 60% of the West Bank. Read more: Claim 48 · The system of control.

Chapter 4h · What has been added since 2009

“But the markets are full” and five more

The 2009 handbook is not the end. Six further claims have since come into circulation or taken on a life of their own — one of them can even be documented as a paid campaign with a budget and reach.

72 · “There is no hunger — just look at the full markets and restaurants.”Evidence rating: refuted — and documented as a paid campaign

What the evidence says: This claim is not an opinion on the internet but an advertising campaign. Between 5 August and 2 September 2025, the Israeli Ministry of Foreign Affairs, through the state advertising agency Lapam, distributed seven videos showing well-stocked market stalls and restaurants — in English, German, Italian and Polish, with a combined total of around 19 million views; 150 million shekels had been budgeted for YouTube and Google (analysis of state procurement and committee documents from 2018 to July 2025). On 22 August 2025 — in the middle of the campaign — the IPC formally determined famine in Gaza City. This is not a contradiction but the core of the method: a market stall with goods does not prove that people are supplied when prices are many times higher and no one has money. The food situation is not determined with pictures but with measurements — malnutrition among children, mortality, household surveys. Read more: Hunger and destruction · The IPC chronology.

73 · “The cement came from the aid deliveries — hence the blockade.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: That Hamas diverted building materials for tunnels is documented and is not disputed here. But that does not justify what followed. After the 2014 war, the UN, together with Israel and the Palestinian Authority, set up the Gaza Reconstruction Mechanism specifically for this purpose: imports under UN supervision, recipients registered, quantities recorded. Cement nevertheless remained on the dual-use list, reconstruction stalled for years, tens of thousands were left without homes — and the same justification later carried the blockade as a whole. Whoever wants to prevent misuse monitors; whoever leaves an entire population without building materials punishes collectively (Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention). Read more: The siege.

74 · “Israel supplies the Palestinians with more water than it agreed to.”Evidence rating: oversimplified

What the evidence says: The point of reference is the water agreement of Oslo II (1995) — intended as a five-year interim arrangement, in force for thirty; the population has more than doubled since. Israel extracts around four fifths of the Mountain Aquifer, which lies beneath the West Bank, and in the Joint Water Committee has a de facto veto: applications for new production wells in the productive Western Aquifer have been rejected almost without exception. The result: 86 litres per person per day in the West Bank compared with around 300 for Israelis and settlers — below the WHO minimum; communities in Area C without a connection buy tanker water at many times the price, and cisterns are destroyed as “illegal”. “Supplying more than agreed” here means: the Palestinians buy back their own water. What the evidence says: Hatred of Jews in the guise of criticism of Israel exists, it is widespread and must be named — without ifs or buts. The blanket equation, however, stems from a definition against whose use its lead author himself has warned: Kenneth Stern – himself Jewish, for decades the antisemitism expert of the American Jewish Committee and in 2004/05 lead author of the draft that the IHRA adopted in 2016 – has warned since 2017 against incorporating it into laws, regulations and university rules, because it would then serve above all to intimidate or suppress criticism of Israel and pro-Palestinian expression; in 2023 he explicitly advised the United Nations not to adopt it (Evidence). In 2021, around 200 scholars of antisemitism and the Holocaust presented the Jerusalem Declaration, which explicitly does not classify criticism of Zionism and of the form of the state as antisemitic. What the evidence says: Restrictions imposed by Hamas have existed and still exist. But the reason why no one can verify independently lies elsewhere: since October 2023, Israel has not allowed foreign journalists into Gaza independently; the Foreign Press Association petitioned the Supreme Court in vain, and Al Jazeera was banned in May 2024. Whoever denies access and then disputes the credibility of the only reporters left makes verification impossible — and then invokes its impossibility. More than 200 Palestinian media workers have been killed. Read more: Journalists killed · Claim 63.

What the evidence says: Iranian support for Hamas — money, weapons, training — is documented. It explains little: Hamas emerged in 1987 from the Palestinian Muslim Brotherhood, not in Tehran; it fell out with Iran over the Syrian civil war from 2012 onwards; and the money that flowed into Gaza from 2018 with Israeli consent came from Qatar. Above all, the chronology does not fit: the occupation has existed since 1967, the Iranian revolution since 1979. Whoever moves the cause to Tehran turns a dispute over land and rights into a question of foreign policy — and no longer has to talk about either. Read more: The handbook · Iran.

The topical chapters: what the handbook reveals about itself

Settlements: three arguments you are not supposed to useChapter 8 of the handbook
