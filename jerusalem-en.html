Jerusalem: the city being taken from its inhabitants

Around 370,000 Palestinians live in East Jerusalem — almost forty percent of the city. They are not citizens but “permanent residents”, with a status that lapses if they live elsewhere for too long; a third of their land has been expropriated, their building permits amount to a fraction, some of their neighbourhoods lie behind the Wall, and their holy sites are the target of a movement that wants to end the status quo. This page tells the story of the eastern part of the city since 1967 — annexation, residency revocation, Silwan, Sheikh Jarrah, al-Aqsa — with the figures of B'Tselem, HaMoked, OCHA and Ir Amim.

The essentials first

The findings of this page

What sets Jerusalem apart from everything else — and what does not

Legally, East Jerusalem is occupied territory like the rest of the West Bank — as stated by the Security Council (Res. 252, 267, 478, 2334) and the ICJ (2004, 2024); hardly any state recognises Israel’s annexation of 1967/1980; since 2017 the USA has recognised Jerusalem as Israel’s capital, but expressly left open the boundaries of Israeli sovereignty there. In practice, it is the place where expropriation, residency revocation, demolition and settler takeover happen house by house — not with tanks, but with zoning plans, court rulings and registration notices. Symbolically, it is the place where the conflict is meant to be turned into a religious war: Ben Gvir’s ascent onto the Haram al-Sharif is not devotion but politics. What Jerusalem is not: a dispute between two equal claims. One side has the bulldozers, the other the demolition orders.

Four figures that underpin this page

The Interior Ministry has revoked the residency status of 14,808 Palestinians since 1967 (HaMoked/B'Tselem, as of 2022). Around 35% of East Jerusalem’s land was expropriated for settlements, while only around 13% is zoned for Palestinian construction (B'Tselem). Around 140,000 Jerusalemites live behind the Wall — in Kufr Aqab and Shuafat camp, registered in the city, cut off from it. And 1 government minister has repeatedly entered the Haram since 2023, and since 2025 also to pray — Ben Gvir, against the status quo of 1967.

Chapter 1

From 1948 to today: the city’s timeline

1948

The city is divided: the west Israeli (Katamon, Talbiya, Baka — neighbourhoods of the expelled Palestinian upper class), the east, with the Old City, Jordanian. Around 30,000 Palestinians lose their homes in the west. → Nakba

10/11 June 1967

Three days after the conquest, the army has the Mughrabi Quarter in front of the Western Wall demolished — 135 houses, two mosques, around 650 residents expelled, to create a plaza. On 28 June, Israel extends its law to East Jerusalem and 28 surrounding villages (70 km²).

1967 · Status

The residents do not become citizens but “permanent residents”, like foreigners with a residence permit — which lapses if the “centre of life” is relocated. Moshe Dayan leaves the Haram al-Sharif to the Waqf administration: the status quo (Jews may visit, not pray).

1980

Jerusalem Law (Basic Law): “Jerusalem, complete and united, is the capital of Israel” (English version of the Hebrew text). Security Council, Resolution 478: “null and void”; the embassies move to Tel Aviv.

8 October 1990

Temple Mount massacre: 17 Palestinians killed by police gunfire; Judge Kama in 1991: the police triggered the violence; no one was charged. → in detail

1993–1996

Oslo sets Jerusalem aside as a “final status issue” — Israel uses the time: sealing off the city to West Bank residents (permit requirement), expanding the rings of settlements (Har Homa 1997), closing Palestinian institutions (Orient House 2001). The tunnel opening in September 1996 sparks days of clashes with more than 60 dead.

28 September 2000

Ariel Sharon visits the Haram with 1,000 police officers — the trigger of the Second Intifada. → Second Intifada

2002–2007

The barrier separates Jerusalem from the West Bank — and cuts off Kufr Aqab, Shuafat camp and parts of Anata: since then, around 140,000 people holding Jerusalem ID cards have lived behind the Wall, without municipal services but obliged to prove their “centre of life”. ICJ 2004: the Wall is unlawful. → Barrier

2004–today

Silwan: The settler organisation Elad runs the archaeological park “City of David” on top of the village, digs tunnels beneath homes and takes over houses in Batn al-Hawa through the Absentees’ Property Law; in al-Bustan, almost the entire neighbourhood is threatened with demolition. 2026: law for an Israeli antiquities authority in the West Bank (3 February 2026).

2009–2021

Sheikh Jarrah: eviction lawsuits by the settler organisation Nahalat Shimon against families resettled there in 1956 by Jordan and UNRWA — on the basis of a 1970 law that allows Jews to reclaim pre-1948 property in East Jerusalem but does not allow Palestinians to do so in the west. The threatened eviction of the al-Kurd family and others sparks the protests in May 2021 that lead to the Gaza war; in 2022 the Supreme Court freezes the eviction — without recognising ownership. → East Jerusalem

14 May 2018

The USA opens its embassy in Jerusalem; on the same day the Israeli army shoots dead around 60 demonstrators at the Gaza border. → Great March

2023–2026

Police minister Ben Gvir ascends the Haram for the first time as a minister on 3 January 2023 and does so again (May 2023, July 2024, August 2025 — with prayer, criticised in cabinet circles as a breach of the status quo); during Ramadan the police restrict access for West Bank residents; Jewish prayers are tolerated. 2025/26: demolition of UNRWA buildings in Sheikh Jarrah under the law of 29 December 2025; OCHA records the highest demolition figures for East Jerusalem in 2024/25 since records began. → UNRWA

Chapter 2

Administering loss: status, land, building permits, demolition

The status that lapses

A “permanent resident” loses their status if they live abroad for seven years or acquire another citizenship — or, under the Interior Ministry’s interpretation since 1995, if their “centre of life” lies outside the city limits, for example in Ramallah or behind the Wall. 14,808 revocations since 1967 (as of 2022); since 2018 also possible as a punishment for “breach of allegiance”. Children of a Jerusalemite father and a mother from the West Bank are not automatically registered. → Law in everyday life: expulsion

The land

Around 35% of the annexed east was expropriated — mostly Palestinian private property — and built over with twelve settlements (Ramot, Gilo, Pisgat Ze'ev, Har Homa …, today around 230,000 settlers); around 52% is green space and “unplanned”, only around 13% is zoned for Palestinian construction — most of it already built up. No new Palestinian neighbourhood has been planned since 1967.

The permit and the demolition

Those who cannot get a permit build without one — and receive a demolition order. Ir Amim estimates around 20,000 housing units without permits; OCHA has recorded hundreds of demolitions every year since 2009, and since 2019 increasingly self-demolitions: the family tears down its own house to avoid the municipality’s demolition costs (tens of thousands of shekels). → Demolitions

The takeover, house by house

Settler organisations (Elad, Ateret Cohanim, Nahalat Shimon) use three routes: the Absentees’ Property Law (property of a Jerusalemite who was abroad in 1967 passes to the state and is leased to settlers), the 1970 reclamation law (only for Jewish pre-1948 property) and purchases through front men. The result: around 3,000 settlers in residential enclaves in the middle of Silwan, Sheikh Jarrah and the Muslim Quarter — guarded by state-funded security staff.

Chapter 3

The Haram al-Sharif: the status quo and who is breaking it

The Dome of the Rock and the al-Aqsa Mosque have stood for 1,300 years on the plateau that Jews revere as the Temple Mount. The status quo of 1967 has kept the site from religious war — until a movement began to shift it step by step.

  • 1What the status quo says: The Jordanian Waqf administers the plateau; Muslims pray there; non-Muslims may visit at fixed times, but not pray; Israel controls the entrances. Agreed in 1967; the 1994 peace treaty with Jordan recognises Jordan’s special role at the holy sites.
  • 2Who wants to break it: The “Temple Mount Faithful” (their announced cornerstone-laying triggered the 1990 unrest in which police shot dead 17 Palestinians), the Temple Institute, which draws up plans for a Third Temple, and since the 2010s politicians from Likud and the Religious Zionists — Ben Gvir, as a minister, prays openly on the plateau in 2025; his ministry oversees the police, which regulate access.
  • 3How access is shifting: Jewish visitor groups with police escort in growing numbers (2024: over 50,000 according to the Temple movements), tolerated prayers, age limits and quotas for Muslims during Ramadan (2024/25: access for West Bank residents limited to a few thousand), police inside the mosque (April 2023, stun grenades and batons in the prayer hall).
  • 4Why this escalates: 1929, 1990, 1996, 2000, 2014, 2017, 2021, 2022 — almost every major wave of violence in Jerusalem began at the Haram. Those who break the status quo know this. → 1929 · → 1990
Chapter 4

The churches: expropriation, spitting, real estate

Jerusalem’s Palestinian Christians have shrunk from around 30,000 (1948) to fewer than 10,000; since 2023 the churches have reported growing attacks — spitting at clergy, vandalism, tax disputes over church land, takeovers of church properties at Jaffa Gate by Ateret Cohanim (the Petra and Imperial hotels, upheld by the Supreme Court in 2022); back in 2015 an arson attack had damaged the Dormition Abbey. Full details on the page Christians in Palestine.
