The Gaza Strip

365 square kilometres, two million people, nineteen years of siege

Gaza is not a news item from 2023. It has been a caravan town for four thousand years, a strip of land that an armistice cut out of the map in 1949, a place where three in four people come from families expelled from what is now Israel. This page tells the story from the beginning, then the reprisals — siege, calorie calculation, fishing limit — and the documented war crimes. And it names the people who researched and recorded all this, often at the risk of their lives.

The war since 2023 and its legal classification

The finding of the UN Commission of Inquiry

The United Nations Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including East Jerusalem, and Israel concluded in September 2025 that Israeli authorities and security forces in the Gaza Strip have committed and are continuing to commit genocide. It established four of the five acts under Article II of the Genocide Convention – killing, serious harm, destructive conditions of life, prevention of births (letters a–d) – and the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, the Palestinians in Gaza. This intent, it said, was “the only reasonable inference” from the totality of the evidence (A/HRC/60/CRP.3, 16 September 2025, paras. 252 and 254; report to the General Assembly A/80/337, paras. 66–70).

Context

Three things you need to know before talking about Gaza

Without these three facts, any debate about Gaza is skewed. They are undisputed and can be looked up in any official statistics.

365 km²is the size of the entire Strip — slightly larger than Munich (311 km²), but with around one and a half times the population
about 2.2 millionpeople lived there before October 2023; about half of them children and adolescents under 18
about 70 %are registered with UNRWA as refugees — people from families who were expelled in 1948 from what is now Israel
The point almost every debate skips

Most people in Gaza do not come from Gaza. They come from Jaffa, Ashkelon (Majdal), Beersheba, from some 250 villages in what is now southern Israel — some less than twenty kilometres from the camps where their descendants live today. Anyone who wants to understand why the Strip is not an ordinary territory has to start here: Gaza is, to a large extent, a refugee camp that has become a city.

Who controls Gaza?

Israel withdrew its settlers and soldiers in 2005. The United Nations, the International Court of Justice and the great majority of states nevertheless continue to classify the Strip as occupied territory — because Israel controls the airspace, the territorial waters, the population registry, customs and six of the seven crossings. The seventh, Rafah, is on the border with Egypt; in May 2024 the Israeli army also took its Gaza side. A government that cannot decide who enters and leaves, who is on the population register and what comes into the territory is not a sovereign government.

Chapter 1 · The history

From caravan town to sealed-off Strip

Gaza is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. That it became a sealed-off strip is not a three-thousand-year-old tragedy but the result of decisions that can be dated.

until 1917Four millennia on the Via Maris

Gaza lies on the Via Maris, the coastal road between Egypt and Mesopotamia — the most important trade route of antiquity. The city is mentioned in Egyptian sources of the second millennium BCE, was one of the five Philistine cities, and belonged to Persia, to Alexander’s empire, to Rome and to Byzantium.

From 637 it was Muslim; the Prophet’s great-grandfather is said to be buried there, and al-Shafi'i, founder of one of the four Sunni schools of law, was born in Gaza in 767. Under the Ottomans, Gaza was the seat of its own district and a centre of the spice and grain trade.

Under the British Mandate, Gaza was the capital of one of six administrative districts of Palestine. This district reached far to the north and east — as far as Majdal (today Ashkelon), Isdud (today Ashdod) and Beersheba. Today’s Strip is only a remnant of it.

1948/49How the Strip came into being: through an armistice line

The Gaza Strip as a geographical unit has existed only since 24 February 1949. On that day, Israel and Egypt signed an armistice agreement on Rhodes. The line where the troops stood became the border. What remained inside was a strip about 41 kilometres long and in places only six kilometres wide.

The number that explains everything

Before the war, about 80,000 people lived in this area. By the end of 1949, some 200,000 refugees had arrived — people from the villages and towns north and east of the new line who were not allowed to return. The population more than tripled within a year, and an agricultural district became the most densely populated refugee area in the world. The eight camps — Jabalia, Shati, Nuseirat, Bureij, Maghazi, Deir al-Balah, Khan Younis and Rafah — still exist today.

Where the people came from — the Gaza district before 1948

The Mandate district of Gaza covered far more than today’s Strip. It included towns and villages that are Israeli today — and from which the families in the camps originate:

  • Al-Majdal (today Ashkelon) — about 10,000 inhabitants before 1948, a centre of weaving.
  • Isdud (today Ashdod), Bayt Jirja, Barbara, Hamama, Najd, Simsim, Burayr and dozens of other villages.

The documentation of these places — location, population, what stands there today — can be found in Walid Khalidi’s All That Remains and in Salman Abu Sitta’s Atlas of Palestine. Both are listed on the page Who documented it.

The last expulsion: Al-Majdal, 1950

A case that is particularly telling because it took place a year and a half after the war — and therefore cannot be explained by fighting.

In Al-Majdal, some 2,600 Palestinians had remained after the war, concentrated in a fenced-off quarter. In the summer and autumn of 1950 they were loaded onto trucks and driven into the Gaza Strip. The Israeli historian Benny Morris reconstructed the process from Israeli archives — the chapter in his volume 1948 and After is simply titled “The Transfer of Al Majdal’s Remaining Arabs to Gaza, 1950”.

Outcome: Al-Majdal became Ashkelon. Those expelled have since lived a few kilometres away in the Strip, with no right of return. There was no compensation and no legal proceeding.

The 1950s: fedayeen attacks and reprisal raids

From the early 1950s, infiltrations into Israel began from the camps — partly attempts by expellees to reach their fields and homes, partly theft, and increasingly also armed attacks on Israeli civilians in which people were killed. Israel responded with reprisal raids on villages and military posts.

The most consequential was the Gaza raid of 28 February 1955 (“Operation Black Arrow”), in which an Israeli unit under Ariel Sharon attacked an Egyptian military base near Gaza; several dozen Egyptian soldiers and Palestinian civilians died. The attack is considered one of the triggers for Egypt’s arms purchase from the Eastern Bloc and thus for the chain of events that led to the Suez War in 1956.

Why this is included here: Under this website’s rule, violence is named in both directions. The attacks from Gaza on Israeli civilians were real and deadly. And the Israeli response mechanism — reprisal against an entire area instead of prosecuting perpetrators — is exactly the pattern that keeps repeating to this day.

1948–1967Under Egyptian administration — and the massacres of 1956

Egypt did not annex the Strip but administered it militarily. The inhabitants received no Egyptian citizenship, only travel documents — a state of limbo whose effects are still felt today.

During the Suez War, Israel occupied the Strip from November 1956 to March 1957. These four months saw two events that every child in Gaza knows and that hardly anyone outside knows:

3.11.1956

Khan Younis. An investigation by UNRWA found 275 Palestinians killed in the town and the adjacent refugee camp.

12.11.1956

Rafah. In the refugee camp there, according to the name lists in the UNRWA special report (A/3212/Add.1), 111 people were killed, 103 of them refugees.

Outcome: There was no prosecution. For a long time Israel denied that these had been anything more than battles. The Maltese-American comics artist Joe Sacco reconstructed the two days through years of interviewing witnesses and published them in 2009 as Footnotes in Gaza — to this day the most detailed account of all.

1967–2005Occupation, bulldozers, settlements

In June 1967, Israel occupied the Strip. What followed was, in its basic features, the same structure as in the West Bank: military administration, military courts, permit requirements, land confiscations — only in an extremely confined space.

  • 1971 — the “pacification”: As head of Southern Command, Ariel Sharon had wide corridors cut through the Jabalia, Shati and Rafah refugee camps so that military vehicles could drive in. According to UN figures, in the summer of 1971 alone more than 15,000 people were affected by demolitions; some 2,400 families lost their homes to make way for about 300 kilometres of patrol roads. More than 10,000 residents of Jabalia and Shati were taken to camps on the Sinai Peninsula — a deportation out of the occupied territory. One of these camps, the “Canada Camp”, still existed decades later.
  • From the 1970s: Israel built 21 settlements in the Strip, most of them in the Gush Katif bloc in the south. In the end, about 8,000 settlers lived there — compared with some 1.3 million Palestinians. The settlements and the military zones closed off to protect them took up a considerable part of the already tiny territory, plus the larger share of the groundwater.
  • 9 December 1987: In Jabalia camp, after a traffic accident in which an Israeli truck killed four Palestinian workers, the First Intifada begins. It starts in Gaza, not in the West Bank.
  • 1994: Following the Oslo Accords, the Palestinian Authority takes over the administration — but not the borders.

1991–2000The closure began before Hamas

A widespread misconception holds that the closure of Gaza was the response to Hamas’s seizure of power in 2007. In fact, it began sixteen years earlier.

  • January 1991, during the Gulf War: Israel imposes a general curfew and, for the first time, a general closure of the occupied territories.
  • March 1993: The exception becomes permanent rule. The permit system is introduced: since then, anyone who wants to leave Gaza — for work, study or medical treatment — has needed Israeli permission. That was before Oslo, before the Palestinian Authority, before any Hamas government.
  • Tens of thousands of people from Gaza who until then had worked in Israel every day gradually lost their jobs. This employment had been the economic foundation of the Strip.

2000–2005Second Intifada: bulldozers at the border

During the Second Intifada, the violence in Gaza shifted to one particular place: the border with Egypt in Rafah. Israel wanted a cleared strip there — the “Philadelphi Route” — and demolished rows of houses to create it.

over 2,500houses were destroyed by the Israeli army in the Gaza Strip between 2000 and 2004 — two thirds of them in Rafah (Human Rights Watch)
166houses in Operation “Rainbow” of 18–24 May 2004 alone
up to 300 mwas the width the buffer zone at the border reached — step by step into the camp

In its report “Razing Rafah” (October 2004), Human Rights Watch concluded that the destruction had been carried out without military necessity in most of the cases examined and was therefore unlawful under international law. During one of these demolitions, on 16 March 2003, the 23-year-old American activist Rachel Corrie died in front of an armoured bulldozer. Outcome: In 2012, an Israeli civil court dismissed her family’s lawsuit and classified her death as an accident during a combat operation.

2005The “disengagement” — and what to make of it

In August and September 2005, Israel withdrew settlers and soldiers. To this day, this is cited as proof that Gaza is free and that the Palestinians squandered their chance. That is why it is worth taking a close look at what the plan itself said and what remained.

Dov Weisglass, then chief of staff to Prime Minister Sharon, said in a 2004 interview with Haaretz about the purpose of the withdrawal that it “supplies the formaldehyde necessary so there is no political process with the Palestinians” (wording as reported by Al Jazeera, 7 October 2004). According to this account, the withdrawal was not a step towards a state but a means of preventing one.

2006/2007The election, the split and the beginning of the siege

On Palestinian politics as a whole – Fatah, Hamas, the internal violence of both authorities, no elections since 2006: Palestinian politics.

In January 2006, elections to the Palestinian Legislative Council were held — rated free and fair by international observers. Hamas won the majority of seats. There followed the boycott of the results by Israel, the USA and the EU, the freezing of tax revenues, the conflict with Fatah and, in June 2007, Hamas’s violent seizure of power in the Strip.

In September 2007, Israel declared Gaza a “hostile territory” and tightened the closure into a complete blockade. It has remained in place ever since — by now for more than nineteen years (as of November 2026).

What happened between the election and the seizure of power

The common short version goes: Hamas won, staged a coup and seized Gaza. The documented version is more complicated — and without it, the rupture cannot be understood.

25.1.2006

The election. International observers, including the Carter Center and the EU, rate it free and fair. Hamas wins 74 of 132 seats. Turnout is about 77 percent.

Spring 2006

The boycott. Israel, the USA and the EU do not recognise the government. Israel withholds the tax revenues collected on behalf of the Palestinian Authority, and aid payments are frozen. Tens of thousands of civil servants receive no salary for months.

April 2008

The revelation. The US magazine Vanity Fair publishes the investigation “The Gaza Bombshell” by David Rose, based on confidential documents and statements by former government officials. According to it, after the election the US government had pursued a plan to arm and fund Fatah forces in order to drive Hamas out of Gaza — coordinated by US Lieutenant General Keith Dayton and Fatah security chief Mohammed Dahlan, with weapons and money channelled through Arab allies. After a Jordanian newspaper had leaked details, Hamas pre-empted it in June 2007.

What follows from this — and what does not. It does not follow that Hamas’s violent seizure of power was thereby justified; it was a rupture, and there have been no elections in Gaza since. It does follow, however, that the narrative “the Palestinians got a chance and voted for Hamas, so they only have themselves to blame” leaves out two things: that the election result was not accepted from outside, and that there was a plan to correct it by force of arms.

What is explicitly stated here as well

Hamas is not a democratic movement: there have been no elections in Gaza since 2007, critics and journalists have been persecuted, and the attack of 7 October 2023 on Israeli civilians was a crime that nothing can justify. This is stated here because this website leaves out no side. It does not change the legal position: A civilian population must not be collectively punished for the acts of a government — Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention.

Chapter 2 · The reprisals

The siege: an administration of scarcity

The blockade is not a fence. It is an administrative system that determines how much food, building material, electricity and movement a population is allowed. Much of it has become public through court rulings and freedom of information requests — mostly won by Israeli organizations.

The calorie paper: when Israel calculated how much Gaza may eat

The Israeli organization Gisha litigated for three and a half years for the release of a Defense Ministry document. On September 5, 2012, the Supreme Court dismissed the state’s appeal; in October 2012 the paper was handed over.

It is a presentation from January 2008 entitled “Food Consumption in the Gaza Strip – Red Lines”. In it, the security establishment, together with the Ministry of Health, calculates the minimum number of calories the population of Gaza needs — broken down by age and sex — and from this, how many trucks of food must be let in each day to prevent malnutrition.

Why it matters: For years the Israeli government stated that the restrictions served security alone. The document shows a management of the bare subsistence minimum of a civilian population. The policy of restricting goods was in force from 2007 to June 2010 and was eased after the international outcry over the attack on the Gaza flotilla — not because of a security situation that had changed.

The fishing limit: the sea as a fence

The Oslo Accords promised Gaza’s fishermen a zone of 20 nautical miles. In reality, the permitted limit over the years mostly lay between three and nine nautical miles and was cut again after every incident — several times to zero. Boats that crossed the invisible line were fired upon, confiscated, or their crews arrested.

The consequence is economically measurable: the productive fishing grounds lie farther out. A fishing industry that could feed a population was thus turned into a loss-making business.

Water, electricity, exit: the everyday figures before October 2023
  • Water: The coastal aquifer is over-exploited and salinized. Before 2023, according to UN figures, around 97 percent of extracted groundwater was considered unfit for drinking by WHO standards.
  • Electricity: For years, four to twelve hours of electricity a day were the norm. Hospitals ran on generators whose fuel likewise required a permit.
  • Exit: Anyone who wanted to travel to East Jerusalem, the West Bank or abroad for treatment needed an Israeli permit. For years the WHO documented denial and delay rates in the double-digit percentage range — including for cancer patients and children.
  • Unemployment: Before October 2023 it stood at about 45 percent, and considerably higher among young people — one of the highest rates in the world.

The economist Sara Roy (Harvard) coined the term de-development for this condition: not underdevelopment, but the systematic dismantling of a functioning economy — in such a way that it can no longer recover by its own strength. She has been describing this since the 1980s, long before Hamas.

What was built despite everything: schools, universities, women doctors

The siege is mostly described in terms of scarcity. Just as important is what has been created in this territory — otherwise all that remains of two million people is a casualty figure.

≈ 97 %literacy rate — one of the highest in the world, higher than in many wealthy countries
19institutions of higher education existed in the Gaza Strip in the 2022/23 academic year: seven universities and twelve colleges
183UNRWA schools — most of them running on double shifts because there is not enough space
  • Al-Aqsa University — traces its origins to a teacher training college founded in 1955, the oldest public institution of higher education in the Strip, most recently around 26,000 students.
  • Islamic University of Gaza, founded in 1978 — the first university in the Strip of all, around 17,900 students in 2022. This is where Refaat Alareer taught.
  • Al-Azhar University – Gaza, opened on October 18, 1991 with 725 students in two faculties — most recently more than 14,000 students in twelve colleges and 80 fields of study.

The double-shift system is no minor detail: it means that two complete schools use the same building, one in the morning, the other in the afternoon. Under blockade conditions no building material could come in for new schools — for years cement was on the list of restricted goods because it can also be used for tunnels and bunkers.

What is left of it since October 2023

All of the Gaza Strip’s universities have been damaged or destroyed, among them Al-Azhar and the Islamic University. For the systematic destruction of educational institutions, the term scholasticide has become established in UN reporting; the evidence is given under Warfare. The territory with one of the highest literacy rates in the world has lost its universities.

The restricted zone inside Gaza: where farmers may not enter their own fields

Part of the siege lies not at the border but within the Strip itself. Along the fence, Israel has designated an access-restricted zone (in English access restricted area). Officially it measures 300 meters; in practice, people approaching were shot at in strips of up to 1,000 to 1,500 meters.

According to surveys by UN OCHA, around 35 percent of the agricultural land of the Gaza Strip lies in the restricted zone as a result — in a territory that cannot feed its population in any case. Farmers who harvested there were regularly fired upon; for years OCHA and Palestinian human rights centers documented deaths and injuries among farmers and scrap collectors.

Why it matters: The blockade is mostly described as a border issue. In fact it shrinks the usable land in the interior — the same effect, without any fence having to be moved. Since 2023 this zone has expanded dramatically; in spring 2025, according to an analysis of Israeli evacuation orders and closed areas, around 70 percent of the Strip was no longer accessible to Palestinians.

The tunnel economy: what emerges when a territory is sealed off

Between 2007 and 2013, a system of hundreds, at times more than a thousand tunnels developed under the border with Egypt near Rafah. Through them came what was not let in above ground: cement, fuel, medicines, car parts, livestock, food — and weapons.

This dual nature is the point. The tunnels were at the same time:

  • the economic lifeline of a sealed-off territory — at times tens of thousands of people worked on them directly or indirectly, and a considerable share of Gaza’s everyday goods came this way;
  • a source of revenue for Hamas, which levied duties on the tunnel trade;
  • and a weapons route, which Israel cites as justification for the closure.

Tunnel construction was life-threatening: over the years, collapses killed hundreds of workers, among them teenagers. From 2013 the Egyptian army systematically destroyed the tunnels — by 2015 more than 2,000 by its own account —, which strangled the Strip economically a second time.

The context that belongs here: A shadow economy with duties, militia financing and deadly working conditions is not a good order. But it did not fall from the sky either — it is the foreseeable result of denying a territory of two million people regular trade in goods. Anyone who criticizes the one must also name the other.

Chapter 3 · The other border

Egypt: the second bolt, less often talked about

Gaza has seven crossings. Israel controls six — the seventh, Rafah, lies on the border with Egypt. An honest account of the siege must also name this part, otherwise the picture is not accurate.

  • Since 2013 Egypt has kept the Rafah crossing mostly closed. In 2015 it was open on 21 days in the entire year.
  • By 2015, the Egyptian army by its own account had destroyed more than 2,000 tunnels under the border — the supply arteries through which Gaza obtained goods under the blockade.
  • On the Egyptian side, the military began creating a buffer zone from July 2013: a decree ordered the clearance of 79 square kilometers, five to seven kilometers deep into the Sinai; affected was the Egyptian town of Rafah with around 78,000 inhabitants. Human Rights Watch documented this in 2015 under the title “Look for Another Homeland” and classified the forced evictions as violating international law.
How to put this in context

Egypt justifies the closure with the fight against armed groups in the Sinai. Human rights organizations counter that a civilian population must not be sealed off for that purpose. Both belong on this page. Responsibility is not equally distributed, however: Israel controls the airspace, coastal waters, population registry, customs and six of the seven crossings, and is the occupying power under international law — with the obligations that follow from that. Egypt is a neighboring state. But anyone who talks about only one of the two borders does not fully describe the situation of the people there.

Chapter 5 · The toll

Hunger and destruction: what two independent measuring systems show

For two things there are internationally standardised measurement methods that do not come from a party to the war: for hunger the IPC, for destruction the satellite analysis by UNOSAT. Both publish their methodology, both have their limits — and both independently of each other arrive at results that are without precedent.

HungerThe IPC scale and the first confirmed famine in the Middle East

The IPC (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification) is a standard established in 2004, backed by FAO, WFP, UNICEF, FEWS NET and the EU. It classifies into five phases, from 1 (minimal) to 5 (catastrophe). Important for understanding the reports:

  • Phase 5 “Catastrophe” is a classification of households or areas and is assigned in every regular analysis.
  • The formal “Famine” classification additionally requires that three hard thresholds are exceeded simultaneously — among them at least 20% of households with an extreme food gap, acute malnutrition of 30% or more, and a death rate of 2 per 10,000 people per day. Only the independent Famine Review Committee can confirm it.
  • An “Alert”, by contrast, is not a classification but an early warning when data are missing. That is why the difference between “famine is imminent” and “famine is confirmed” is not wordplay but a methodological difference.

The three categories without which no number can be understood
  1. Direct war deaths (counted). People who died from bombs, shelling or gunfire and were registered by name. This is the Ministry of Health’s figure and the basis of this website’s name database.
  2. Direct war deaths (not registered). Anyone who lies under rubble, never reaches a hospital or dies in an area without a functioning administration does not appear on any list. This gap can be measured statistically — and it is large.
  3. Indirect deaths. People who died of disease, malnutrition, lack of medical care, contaminated water, cold or interrupted treatment. A dialysis patient without dialysis, a premature baby without an incubator, a cancer patient without therapy — these people die of the war, but not by a weapon. In technical language they are called excess deaths, excess mortality.

Category 3The indirect deaths — the most difficult number

This is where things become methodologically delicate, and so this section sets out exactly what is documented and what is not.

What was measured

The only empirical survey of indirect deaths is the household survey in Lancet Global Health: 8,540 non-violent excess deaths up to 5 January 2025, with a confidence interval of 4,540 to 12,500. In total the survey found 16,300 non-violent deaths (12,300–20,200); the 8,540 are the share above the mortality expected before the war.

This figure is based on a survey in Gaza itself, not on a borrowed factor. At the same time it is conservative: it ends in January 2025, before the famine that the Famine Review Committee formally confirmed for Gaza City in August 2025, and before the at least 240 documented deaths from malnutrition.

The figure 186,000 — what it is and what it is not

In July 2024, The Lancet published a letter by Rasha Khatib, Martin McKee and Salim Yusuf under the title “Counting the dead in Gaza: difficult but essential”. In it, the authors took the then official 37,396 direct deaths and added four indirect deaths for every direct death. Result: over 186,000. This figure went around the world.

Extrapolation, not measurement What needs to be said about it:

  • It is correspondence, not a peer-reviewed study. The Lancet explicitly labels letters as generally not peer-reviewed.
  • The 4:1 ratio comes from the report Global Burden of Armed Violence of 2008, which analysed other conflicts — it is not a measurement in Gaza.
  • Experts, including Michael Spagat, have criticised this transfer. And the later empirical measurement in the same journal found a considerably lower ratio of indirect to direct mortality than 4:1.

Why it appears here anyway: because it is in circulation and people need to know where it comes from. Why this page does not use it as a figure: because an extrapolation using a factor from other wars cannot replace a survey — and because the measured figures are strong enough on their own. Anyone who wants to defend the 186,000 has to defend the factor; anyone who cites the surveyed 75,200 plus 8,540 has nothing to defend.

The scenario projections by LSHTM and Johns Hopkins

A separate research project by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine and the Johns Hopkins Center for Humanitarian Health, titled Crisis in Gaza: Scenario-Based Health Impact Projections, calculates the expected deaths broken down by cause — injuries, infectious diseases, maternal and child mortality and non-communicable diseases — each for three scenarios: ceasefire, status quo and escalation.

These are projections, not counts — their value lies in showing which causes of death increase under which scenario. It is precisely this breakdown that is missing from the public debate, which argues almost exclusively over a single overall figure.

What can be said in summary
  • The officially counted figure is the lower bound — according to the studies it lies around 35 to 41 % below the estimated values (household survey 34.7 %, capture-recapture 41 %).
  • The studies already arrived at around 75,000 to 78,000 violent deaths in early 2025, plus several thousand indirect ones — and the Max Planck Institute’s update puts the total at over 100,000 by October 2025.
  • All these studies end before or shortly after the 2025 famine. The indirect deaths of 2025 and 2026 have not yet been surveyed — by contrast, officially counted up to 4 November 2026 are 74,618 deaths and 175,920 injuries (Ministry of Health, as reported by OCHA).
  • The circulating figure of 186,000 is an extrapolation using a factor from other wars, not a survey in Gaza.

The honest answer to the question “how many?” is therefore: more than officially counted, over 80,000 by early 2025 according to the household survey (75,200 violent and 8,540 additional non-violent deaths), over 100,000 by autumn 2025 according to demographic projection — and for the period after that there is not yet any reliable survey.

Comics journalist. For Footnotes in Gaza (2009) he interviewed survivors over several years and reconstructed the two days of Khan Younis and Rafah in 1956 — events that had disappeared from almost all history books. Earlier, Palestine (1993/96), from the First Intifada.

Norman Finkelstein

Political scientist, son of two Holocaust survivors. Gaza: An Inquest into Its Martyrdom (2018) examines the major investigative reports — Goldstone, the UN commissions — sentence by sentence for their evidence. A sharply argued but source-saturated work.

Amira Hass Haaretz

Israeli journalist, daughter of Holocaust survivors. She moved to Gaza in the early 1990s and lived there for years as the only Israeli correspondent. Drinking the Sea at Gaza (1996) is the result. She is still reporting today.

Ramzy Baroud

Palestinian journalist from the Nuseirat camp. My Father Was a Freedom Fighter: Gaza's Untold Story (2010) tells the history of the Strip through his father’s biography — history from below, from a family that was expelled from the village of Beit Daras in 1948.

Refaat Alareer 1979–2023

Professor of literature at the Islamic University of Gaza, editor of Gaza Writes Back — short stories by young writers from the Strip. He taught a generation to write down their own story. Killed in an Israeli airstrike on 6 December 2023, together with several relatives. His poem “If I must die” went around the world.

Atef Abu Saif

Writer from Jabalia. The Drone Eats with Me is his diary from the 2014 war; during the war from 2023 onwards he again kept a diary, which was published in several languages.

PCHR & Al Mezan Gaza

The two Palestinian human rights centres in the Strip. Since the 1990s they have maintained case-by-case databases with name, date, place and circumstances — the basis of almost all later UN reports. The PCHR was co-founded by Raji Sourani, who was imprisoned both under Israeli occupation and under Palestinian rule.

Gisha Israel

Israeli legal organisation for freedom of movement. It obtained the calorie paper through litigation and maintains fact sheets on crossings, lists of goods and exit permits.

Forensic Architecture London

Research group at Goldsmiths College. It reconstructs individual attacks from video footage, satellite images, sound analysis and witness testimony — including the case of five-year-old Hind Rajab (January 2024).

The journalists in Gaza

Since October 2023, according to the count of the Committee to Protect Journalists, more media workers have been killed than in any other conflict since records began. Because the international press has no free access, almost all visual material comes from Palestinian journalists on the ground. They are the source — and at the same time those affected.

The starting point Where do the people who live in Gaza come from?

Most of them do not come from Gaza. They come from Jaffa, Ashkelon (Majdal), Beersheba and some 250 villages in what is now southern Israel — some less than twenty kilometres from the camps in which their descendants live today.

Anyone who wants to understand why the Strip is not an ordinary territory has to start here: to a large extent, Gaza is a refugee camp that has become a city.

1948/49 Which single figure best explains the situation?

Before the war, about 80,000 people lived in this area. By the end of 1949 some 200,000 refugees had arrived — people from the villages and towns north and east of the new line who were not allowed to return.

The population more than tripled within a single year, and an agricultural district became the most densely populated refugee area in the world.

The eight camps still exist today: Jabalia, Shati, Nuseirat, Bureij, Maghazi, Deir al-Balah, Khan Younis and Rafah.

Read more: The figure that explains everything
International law Israel withdrew in 2005 — so why is Gaza still considered occupied?

Because occupation is measured by effective control, not by the presence of settlers. The United Nations, the International Court of Justice and the great majority of states continue to classify the Strip as occupied territory — because Israel controls the airspace, the territorial waters, the population registry, customs and six of the seven crossings.

Read more: Who controls Gaza?
January 2008 What does the so-called “calorie paper” say?

A Defence Ministry presentation entitled “Food Consumption in the Gaza Strip – Red Lines”. In it, the security establishment, together with the Ministry of Health, calculates the minimum number of calories the population needs — broken down by age and sex — and from that, how many trucks must be let in each day to prevent malnutrition.

Why it matters: For years the government stated that the restrictions served security alone. The document shows a management of a civilian population’s subsistence minimum.

It was obtained by the Israeli organisation Gisha — after three and a half years of litigation, until the Supreme Court dismissed the state’s appeal on 5 September 2012.

Read more: The siege
The second bolt What role does Egypt play?

Gaza has seven crossings. Israel controls six — the seventh, Rafah, is on the border with Egypt. An honest account of the siege must name this part too, otherwise the picture is wrong.

  • Since 2013 Egypt has kept Rafah closed most of the time — in 2015 the crossing was open on 21 days in the entire year.
  • By 2015, by its own account, the Egyptian army had destroyed more than 2,000 tunnels — the supply arteries beneath the blockade.
  • From July 2013 the military had a buffer zone created: a decree ordered the clearance of 79 square kilometres.

Methodology Why is even the official figure still too low?

Because it captures only one of three categories:

  • Direct war deaths, counted. Recorded by name — this is the Ministry of Health’s figure and the basis of this website’s database of names.
  • Direct war deaths, not recorded. Anyone who lies under rubble, never reaches a hospital or dies in an area without a functioning administration appears on no list. This gap can be measured statistically — and it is large.
  • Indirect deaths. People who died of disease, malnutrition, lack of care, contaminated water or interrupted treatment — a dialysis patient without dialysis, a premature baby without an incubator, a woman with cancer without therapy.
Read more: The three categories
Measurement How do we know that there was starvation in Gaza — independently of the parties to the war?

Through two internationally standardised measurement systems that do not originate with a party to the war: the IPC for hunger and the satellite analysis of UNOSAT for destruction. Both publish their methodology, both have their limits — and both arrive independently of each other at unprecedented results.

The IPC is a standard established in 2004, supported by FAO, WFP, UNICEF, FEWS NET and the EU. It classifies in five phases, from 1 (minimal) to 5 (catastrophe).

Integrity What does this page expressly not claim?

This page works with documented figures and states what is not documented — even when the more cautious figure is the less convenient one. For every figure it names the body that collected it and the reference date, it presents opposing positions, and it separates what was counted from what was estimated.

That is not distrust of those affected, but what makes a figure robust when someone disputes it.
